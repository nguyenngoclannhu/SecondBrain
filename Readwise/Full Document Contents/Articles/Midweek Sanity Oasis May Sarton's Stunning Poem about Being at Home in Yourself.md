### [The Work of Happiness: May Sarton’s Stunning Poem About Being at Home in Yourself](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=56af95c29c&e=f6b8863c15)

[![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/maysarton_collectedpoems.jpg?fit=320%2C480)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=2e7c7372ab&e=f6b8863c15)

In a culture predicated on the perpetual pursuit of happiness, as if it were a fugitive on the loose, it can be hard to discern what *having* happiness actually feels like, how it actually lives in us. Willa Cather came consummately close in her definition of happiness as the feeling of being [“dissolved into something complete and great”](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=e544491fc2&e=f6b8863c15) — a definition consonant with Iris Murdoch’s lovely notion of [unselfing](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=56c2102444&e=f6b8863c15). And yet happiness is equally a matter of how we inhabit the self — how we make ourselves at home in our own singular lives, in the dwelling-places of our own experience. 

That is what [May Sarton](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=7592fa761d&e=f6b8863c15) (May 3, 1912–July 16, 1995), who has written so movingly about [unhappiness and its cure](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=66dcb9b279&e=f6b8863c15), explores in her poem “The Work of Happiness,” included in her indispensable [***Collected Poems: 1930–1993***](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=00835f395e&e=f6b8863c15) ([*public library*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=a53e6c4fc8&e=f6b8863c15)).

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)**THE WORK OF HAPPINESS**  
> *by May Sarton*

> I thought of happiness, how it is woven  
> Out of the silence in the empty house each day  
> And how it is not sudden and it is not given  
> But is creation itself like the growth of a tree.  
> No one has seen it happen, but inside the bark  
> Another circle is growing in the expanding ring.  
> No one has heard the root go deeper in the dark,  
> But the tree is lifted by this inward work  
> And its plumes shine, and its leaves are glittering.

> So happiness is woven out of the peace of hours  
> And strikes its roots deep in the house alone:  
> The old chest in the corner, cool waxed floors,  
> White curtains softly and continually blown  
> As the free air moves quietly about the room;  
> A shelf of books, a table, and the white-washed wall —  
> These are the dear familiar gods of home,  
> And here the work of faith can best be done,  
> The growing tree is green and musical.

> For what is happiness but growth in peace,  
> The timeless sense of time when furniture  
> Has stood a life’s span in a single place,  
> And as the air moves, so the old dreams stir  
> The shining leaves of present happiness?  
> No one has heard thought or listened to a mind,  
> But where people have lived in inwardness  
> The air is charged with blessing and does bless;  
>  Windows look out on mountains and the walls are kind.

<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left"><a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=2e7c7372ab&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><img align="right" height="auto" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/maysarton\_collectedpoems.jpg?fit=320%2C480" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; display:inline; width:150px; margin:5px 0 10px 30px" width="150"/></a></p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">In a culture predicated on the perpetual pursuit of happiness, as if it were a fugitive on the loose, it can be hard to discern what <em>having</em> happiness actually feels like, how it actually lives in us. Willa Cather came consummately close in her definition of happiness as the feeling of being <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=e544491fc2&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">“dissolved into something complete and great”</a> — a definition consonant with Iris Murdoch’s lovely notion of <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=56c2102444&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">unselfing</a>. And yet happiness is equally a matter of how we inhabit the self — how we make ourselves at home in our own singular lives, in the dwelling-places of our own experience. </p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">That is what <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=7592fa761d&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">May Sarton</a> (May 3, 1912–July 16, 1995), who has written so movingly about <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=66dcb9b279&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">unhappiness and its cure</a>, explores in her poem “The Work of Happiness,” included in her indispensable <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=00835f395e&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><strong><em>Collected Poems: 1930–1993</em></strong></a> (<a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=a53e6c4fc8&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><em>public library</em></a>).</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left"><a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=e95a3f3fe4&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><img height="auto" src="https://mcusercontent.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/7c103a9d-2ca8-fe0f-da6d-fb85752d157c.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; display:inline; width:100%" width="100%"/></a></p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0">
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/><strong>THE WORK OF HAPPINESS</strong><br/><em>by May Sarton</em></p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left">I thought of happiness, how it is woven<br/>Out of the silence in the empty house each day<br/>And how it is not sudden and it is not given<br/>But is creation itself like the growth of a tree.<br/>No one has seen it happen, but inside the bark<br/>Another circle is growing in the expanding ring.<br/>No one has heard the root go deeper in the dark,<br/>But the tree is lifted by this inward work<br/>And its plumes shine, and its leaves are glittering.</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left">So happiness is woven out of the peace of hours<br/>And strikes its roots deep in the house alone:<br/>The old chest in the corner, cool waxed floors,<br/>White curtains softly and continually blown<br/>As the free air moves quietly about the room;<br/>A shelf of books, a table, and the white-washed wall —<br/>These are the dear familiar gods of home,<br/>And here the work of faith can best be done,<br/>The growing tree is green and musical.</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left">For what is happiness but growth in peace,<br/>The timeless sense of time when furniture<br/>Has stood a life’s span in a single place,<br/>And as the air moves, so the old dreams stir<br/>The shining leaves of present happiness?<br/>No one has heard thought or listened to a mind,<br/>But where people have lived in inwardness<br/>The air is charged with blessing and does bless;<br/>      Windows look out on mountains and the walls are kind.</p>
</blockquote>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Complement with Bertrand Russell on <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=a4fb59aee7&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">the secret of happiness</a> and Kurt Vonnegut on <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=f12ce606bc&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">the one word it comes down to</a>, then revisit Sarton’s poem <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=c1832ed90c&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">“Meditation in Sunlight”</a> and her magnificent <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=2b1f8f8d93&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">ode to solitude</a>.</p>
