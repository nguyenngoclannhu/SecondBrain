### [How Two Souls Can Interact with One Another: Simone de Beauvoir on Love and Friendship](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=13ba775d37&e=f6b8863c15)

[![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/debeauvoir_memoir.jpg?fit=320%2C491)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=67e2282561&e=f6b8863c15)

It is in relationships that we discover both our depths and our limits, there that we anneal ourselves and transcend ourselves, there that we are hurt the most and there that we find the most healing.

But despite what a crucible of our emotional and spiritual lives relationships are — or perhaps precisely because of it — they can be riddling and nebulous, destabilizing in their fluidity and ambiguity, leaving us grasping for the comforting solidity of categories and labels. The ancient Greeks, in their pioneering effort to order the chaos of the cosmos, neatly taxonomized them into filial love (the kind we feel for siblings, children, parents, and friends), *eros* (the love of lovers), and *agape* (the deepest, purest, most impersonal and spiritual love). After the Enlightenment discounted all love as a malfunction of reason, the Romantics reclaimed it and revised the ancient taxonomy into a hierarchy, under the tyranny of which we still live, placing eros at the pinnacle of human existence. And yet our deepest relationships — the ones in which we both become most fully ourselves and are most emboldened to change — tend to [elude the commonplace classifications](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=c9c183c81c&e=f6b8863c15) and to [shape-shift across the span of life](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=eeb63c9d54&e=f6b8863c15).

[![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/simonedebeauvoir_1946-1.jpg)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=e09deb73bd&e=f6b8863c15)

Simone de Beauvoir, 1946 (Photograph: Henri Cartier-Bresson)

[Simone de Beauvoir](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=67fbcf2809&e=f6b8863c15) (January 9, 1908–April 14, 1986) was only nineteen when she wielded her uncommon intellect at these questions on the pages of her journal, later published as [***Diary of a Philosophy Student***](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=e4c709a793&e=f6b8863c15) ([*public library*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=0928ab233d&e=f6b8863c15)). In between composing [her resolutions for a life worth living](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=308c593329&e=f6b8863c15), Beauvoir began thinking seriously about the nature of love, its dialogue with her own nature, what she may want of it and what it may demand of her — “in brief, how souls can interact with one another.” In the midst of an intellectual infatuation with a young man who would go on to become an eminent philosopher himself — not the one she would eventually marry in a convention-breaking union of minds — she examines the substance of the feeling:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)To say that I love him, what does that mean? Does the word itself have a meaning?

Questioning the tangle of idolization and desire that masquerades as love, she grows suspicious of the very concept of personal love as an absurdity against the backdrop of the largest love we can carry:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)When you love beings… not for their intelligence, etc., but for what they have in their very depths, for their soul… you love them equally: they are entireties, perfect inasmuch as they are (to be = perfection). Why then is there this desire to get closer? To know them, and thus to love them more perfectly for what they really are. What is surprising is not that we love them all, but rather that we prefer one of them.

Invoking the love she feels for her friends, the sum total of them, she writes:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)Something sharp runs through me which is my love for them… This is not intellectual love. This is a love for souls, from all of me towards all of them in their entirety.

Over and over she returns to the elemental question:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)What then is love? Not much, not much; I come back to this idea. Sensitivity, imagination, fatigue, and this effort to depend on another; the taste for the mystery of the other and the need to admire… What is worthwhile, is friendship… this profound mutual confidence between [two people], and this joy of knowing that the other exists.

[![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/bigwolflittlewolf10.jpg?w=1200)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=bd08badc04&e=f6b8863c15)

Art by Olivier Tallec from [*Big Wolf & Little Wolf*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=48581426a4&e=f6b8863c15) by Nadine Brun-Cosme — a poignant modern fable about how friendship anchors and transforms us.

Drawing on Hegel’s philosophy of freedom, in which for any conscious subject to be free means freeing the other, she arrives at a “formula” for the ideal friendship: “absolute reciprocity and the identity of consciousness.” The cultural ideal of romantic love, on the other hand, replaces this “absolute reciprocity” with engulfment and sublimation of one self into the other. She writes:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)It seems to me that love should not make all else disappear but should simply tint it with new nuances; I would like a love that accompanies me through life, not that absorbs all my life.

This, of course, is Rilke’s [model of a perfect relationship](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=a6da9f481e&e=f6b8863c15) — one in which “the highest task of a bond between two people [is] that each should stand guard over the solitude of the other” — consonant with Octavio Paz’s lovely definition of love as [“a knot made of two intertwined freedoms.”](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=5c32372158&e=f6b8863c15)

Beauvoir ultimately found it not in romantic love but in the deepest friendship of her life — that with Zaza, her childhood best friend.

A year older than her and also enamored of books, Zaza was the only one with whom the young Simone could have “real conversations.” In [***Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter***](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=04b08953f9&e=f6b8863c15) ([*public library*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=59b06dc889&e=f6b8863c15)) — the first volume of her autobiography, largely a loving memorial to this formative relationship — she would write of talking to Zaza:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)My tongue was suddenly loosened, and a thousand bright suns began blazing in my breast; radiant with happiness.

[![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/07/BlueBird_friend.jpg)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=a8d2a4496d&e=f6b8863c15)

Card from [*An Almanac of Birds: 100 Divinations for Uncertain Days*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=3041fde7a6&e=f6b8863c15), also available as a [stand-alone print](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=7462807411&e=f6b8863c15) and as [stationery cards](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=3a16ab83d0&e=f6b8863c15).

When Zaza’s dress caught fire and charred her leg to the bone, she endured the long convalescence valiantly, then went on to climb trees and do cartwheels, to play the piano and the violin. Beauvoir relays a moment radical in the context of early twentieth-century French bourgeoise society, emblematic of Zaza’s defiant spirit and playful disdain for convention:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)One year at a music recital [Zaza] did something while she was playing the piano which was very nearly scandalous. The hall was packed. In the front rows were the pupils in their best frocks, curled and ringleted and beribboned, who were awaiting their turn to show off their talents. Behind them sat the teachers and tutors in stiff black silk bodices, wearing white gloves. At the back of the hall were seated the parents and their guests. Zaza, resplendent in blue taffeta, played a piece which her mother thought was too difficult for her; she always had to scramble through a few of the bars: but this time she played it perfectly, and, casting a triumphant glance at [her mother], put out her *tongue* at her! All the little girls’ ringlets trembled with apprehension and the teachers’ faces froze into disapproving masks. But when Zaza came down from the platform her mother gave her such a light-hearted kiss that no one dare reprimand her. For me this exploit surrounded her with a halo of glory. Although I was subject to laws, to conventional behaviour, to prejudice, I nevertheless liked anything novel, sincere, and spontaneous. I was completely won over by Zaza’s vivacity and independence of spirit.

This strength of spirit, this defiance of the givens, is what the young Simone most admired about her friend — it emboldened her to defy convention in her own life.

Part of the unexamined convention Beauvoir had internalized growing up was the belief that “in a well-regulated human heart friendship occupies an honourable position, but it has neither the mysterious splendour of love, nor the sacred dignity of filial devotion.” And yet through her relationship with Zaza, she came to question this limiting “hierarchy of the emotions” and to see friendship as the deepest stratum of connection. “I loved Zaza with an intensity which could not be accounted for by any established set of rules and conventions,” she would reflect decades later.

![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/Black-HeadedGrosbeak_friend.jpg)

Card from [*An Almanac of Birds: 100 Divinations for Uncertain Days*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=2474d77c1f&e=f6b8863c15), also available as a [stand-alone print](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=6e3c246da3&e=f6b8863c15) and as [stationery cards](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=e74e57f4d8&e=f6b8863c15).

It was only in Zaza’s absence — absences inflicted by their families and school schedules and the general fractures of continuity that life presents — that Beauvoir came to grasp the importance, the consolation, the salvation of her friend’s presence:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)So total had been my ignorance of the workings of the heart that I hadn’t thought of telling myself: ‘I miss her.’ I needed her presence to realize how much I needed her. This was a blinding revelation. All at once, conventions, routines, and the careful categorizing of emotions were swept away and I was overwhelmed by a flood of feeling that had no place in any code. I allowed myself to be uplifted by that wave of joy which went on mounting inside me, as violent and fresh as a waterfalling cataract, as naked, beautiful, and bare as a granite cliff.

In her diary, she recounts one such reunion during her freshman year as a philosophy student:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)I found Zaza again! All last year and during this vacation, I believed that she was far, very far from me. And there she was infinitely close by and now we are going to be true friends. Oh! What a beautiful meaning this word has! Never have we spoken so, and I was not even hoping that it could happen — but why, too, never believe in happiness… Let us bring our two solitudes together!… When I had left her, I experienced one of the most beautiful hours of my life, my love and my friendship both greater from their union.

Beauvoir was discovering deep friendship as safer and more resilient than romance, free from “the great hatreds of love, the irremediable pride, the passionate ruptures, the mutual tortures,” never “introducing jealousy, demands, and doubts.” To have what the ancient Celts [called *anam cara*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=4503d88824&e=f6b8863c15) — “soul friend” — asks everything of us, invites all [the parts we live with](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=20ce8b4951&e=f6b8863c15) and urges us to show up whole, yet demands nothing.

Looking back on her life, Beauvoir reflects:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)I didn’t require Zaza to have any such definite feelings about me: it was enough to be her best friend. The admiration I felt for her did not diminish me in my own eyes. Love is not envy. I could think of nothing better in the world than being myself, and loving Zaza.

Midway through Beauvoir’s sophomore year, Zaza died suddenly and mysteriously — an illness swift and merciless as an owl. She was 21. Amid the savage grief, Beauvoir turned even more sharply toward philosophy, seeking [its eternal consolations](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=2f10950cf8&e=f6b8863c15). Across the sweep of the years and decades, Zaza’s inextinguishable presence never left her life. (“No one you love is ever dead,” Ernest Hemingway wrote around that time in [a letter of consolation](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=0531047049&e=f6b8863c15) to an inconsolable friend.) Loving Zaza had ignited Beauvoir’s becoming, setting her on the course of who she would become — one of humanity’s most daring breakers of convention, her ideas reaching into the depths of her time, shaping the times to come, touching the lives of generations of strangers the way a true friendship does. Touching mine. Perhaps touching yours.

### [Carl Jung on Creativity](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=1da5148319&e=f6b8863c15)

[![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/jung_soul.jpg?fit=320%2C508)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=36b70e70c3&e=f6b8863c15)

The question of what it takes to create — to make something of beauty and substance that touches other lives across space and time — is one of the deepest, oldest questions, perhaps because the answer to it is so unbearably simple: everything. We bring everything we are and everything we have lived to every smallest creative act — every experience, every dream, every memory, every unremembered impression, every unconsciously absorbed influence. The great bewilderment is that we can only access a fraction of our own everythingness — most of it dwells in the recesses of the mind and the psyche, below the level of our surface awareness. Creativity is the periscope through which the unconscious looks out onto the world and renders what it sees. The rendering is what we call art, and it is as much a picture of the seer as of the seen.

In the middle of the world’s most destructive war, [Carl Jung](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=280c3c6a80&e=f6b8863c15) took up this elemental mystery of the creative spirit in a chapter of his 1939 book [***Modern Man in Search of a Soul***](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=dbc653454b&e=f6b8863c15) ([*public library*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=e18b389dc9&e=f6b8863c15)).

Living in that liminal epoch between the age of mysticism, when creativity was considered a divine gift superintended by muses and shamans, and the age of science, which aimed its forceps and fMRIs at regions of the brain hoping to locate the mind and microscopize the soul, Jung believed that “the human psyche is the womb of all the sciences and arts,” that the unconscious is “the necessary undercurrent of all creativity,” and that to understand how a work of art comes into being is to behold “the warp and weft of the mind in all its amazing intricacy.” Though rigorous and systematic in his approach, he was never seduced by the reductionism science often tends toward, including his own young science of psychology, once writing to a colleague:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)The creative imagination is the only primordial phenomenon accessible to us, the real Ground of the psyche, the only immediate reality… All other realities are derived from and indirectly revealed by it, actually with the artificial aid named science.

[![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/03/carljung.jpg?w=680)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=a3ae967ffd&e=f6b8863c15)

Carl Jung

Long before psychologist Jerome Bruner itemized [the six pillars of creativity](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=6f427ef4c4&e=f6b8863c15) and neurologist Oliver Sacks contemplated [its three essential elements](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=7fd97538b5&e=f6b8863c15), Jung foregrounds his perspective with a lucid caveat about the limitations of reason in comprehending the unconscious. In a sentiment evocative of Virginia Woolf’s astute observation that [“one can’t write directly about the soul [because] looked at, it vanishes,”](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=df2fe5b387&e=f6b8863c15) Jung writes:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)The creative aspect of life, which finds its clearest expression in art, baffles all attempts at rational formation. Any reaction to stimulus may be causally explained; but the creative act, which is the absolute antithesis of mere reaction, will forever elude the human understanding. It can only be described in its manifestations; it can be obscurely sensed, but never wholly grasped.

Jung approaches this acausal mystery of creativity by delineating two distinct modes of creation — the psychological and the visionary — each drawing on different aspects of existence and making different demands on us. He considers the first:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)The psychological mode deals with materials drawn from the realm of human consciousness — for instance, with the lessons of life, with emotional shocks, the experience of passion and the crises of human destiny in general — all of which go to make up the conscious life of man, and his feeling life in particular. This material is psychically assimilated by the poet, raised from the commonplace to the level of poetic experience, and given an expression which forces the reader to greater clarity and depth of human insight by bringing fully into his consciousness what [he](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=2bfa078087&e=f6b8863c15) ordinarily evades and overlooks or senses only with a feeling of dull discomfort. The poet’s work is an interpretation and illumination of the contents of consciousness, of the ineluctable experiences of human life with its eternally recurrent sorrow and joy. He leaves nothing over for the psychologist… Such themes go to make up the lot humankind; they repeat themselves millions of times… No obscurity whatever surrounds them, for they fully explain themselves.

Jung contrasts this with the visionary mode of creation, in which the conditions are reversed:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)[In the visionary mode] the experience that furnishes the material for artistic expression is no longer familiar. It is a strange something that derives its existence from the hinterland of man’s mind — that suggests the abyss of time separating us from pre-human ages, or evokes a super-human world of contrasting light and darkness. It is a primordial experience which surpasses man’s understanding, and to which he is therefore in danger of succumbing. The value and the force of the experience are given by its enormity. It arises form timeless depths… that in every way exceed the grasp of human feeling and comprehension [which] makes quite other demands upon the powers of the artist than do the experiences of the foreground of life. These never rend the curtain that veils the cosmos; they never transcend the bounds of the humanly possible, and for this reason are readily shaped to the demands of art, no matter how great a shock to the individual they may be. But the primordial experiences rend from top to bottom the curtain upon which is painted the picture of an ordered world, and allow a glimpse into the unfathomed abyss of what has not yet become.

[![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/thelittleprince_morgan10.jpg?w=680)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=847ed43930&e=f6b8863c15)

One of Antoine de Saint-Exupéry’s [original watercolors for *The Little Prince*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=d646e3b110&e=f6b8863c15)

While the psychological mode reflects and reckons with the realities of everyday life, the visionary mode is closer to the realm of [dreams](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=b814a3bfd0&e=f6b8863c15), which Jung considered “eclipses of consciousness” — those bewilderments that confuse our sense of reality and beckon interpretation, the way a great poem might, effecting “the frightening revelation of abysses that defy the human understanding.” He writes:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)In dealing with the psychological mode of artistic creation, we never need ask ourselves what the material consists of or what it means. But this question forces itself upon us as soon as we come to the visionary mode of creation. We are astonished, taken aback, confused, put on our guard or even disgusted — and we demand commentaries and explanations. We are reminded in nothing of everyday, human life, but rather of dreams, nighttime fears and dark recesses of the mind that we sometimes sense with misgiving.

> […]

> A great work of art is like a dream; for all its apparent obviousness it does not explain itself and is never unequivocal. A dream never says: “You ought,” or: “This is the truth.” It presents an image in much the same way as nature allows a plant to grow, and we must draw our own conclusions.

[![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/davidthedreamer10.jpg?w=680)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=9151e2a54b&e=f6b8863c15)

Illustration by Tom Seidmann-Freud from [*David the Dreamer: His Book of Dreams*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=2a4f3b32ec&e=f6b8863c15), 1922.

The psychological mode of creation concerns itself with an overt rendering of human emotion as we know it and experience it, but the visionary reaches beyond [the horizon of our self-knowledge](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=9058550bf7&e=f6b8863c15) and into those depths that only the tendrils of [our intuitions](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=4362ada6ee&e=f6b8863c15) every touch. Because what we find there may so alarm us, may so contradict our conscious self-image, we tend to doubt our discoveries and retreat behind “the armor of reason” to dismiss them. Jung writes:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)Human passion falls within the sphere of conscious experience, while the subject of the vision lies beyond it. Through our feelings we experience the known, but our intuitions point to things that are unknown and hidden — that by their very nature are secret. If ever they become conscious, they are intentionally kept back and concealed, for which reason they have been regarded from earlier times as mysterious, uncanny, and deceptive.

Holding up the poet in that Baldwinian sense (“The poets (by which I mean all artists),” Baldwin [wrote](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=4f41f6ff73&e=f6b8863c15), “are finally the only people who know the truth about us. Soldiers don’t. Statesmen don’t… Only poets.”), Jung adds:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)In our midst, the poet now and then catches sight of the figures that people the night-world — the spirits, demons and gods. He knows that a purposiveness out-reaching human ends is the life-giving secret for man; he has a presentiment of incomprehensible happenings in the pleroma.

[![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/BarredOwl_margin.jpg)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=4890d41edd&e=f6b8863c15)

Art from [*An Almanac of Birds: 100 Divinations for Uncertain Days*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=ea20ed0976&e=f6b8863c15), also available as a [stand-alone print](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=f5df9b207d&e=f6b8863c15).

It is not only great artists, he observes, who can access those hidden places but also “the seers, prophets, leaders and enlighteners.” A year after science split the atom and a century before it began intimating that [consciousness may be as old as the universe and fundamental to it](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=36d4a0a173&e=f6b8863c15), Jung writes:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)Is there something more purposeful than electrons? Do we delude ourselves in thinking that we possess and command our own souls? And is that which science calls the “psyche” not merely a question-mark arbitrarily confined within the skull, but rather a door that opens upon the human world from a world beyond, now and again allowing strange and unseizable potencies to act upon man and to remove him, as if upon the wings of the night, from the level of common humanity to that of a more than personal vocation?

That more-than-personal aspect of visionary work is what Jung calls “the collective unconscious.” It is where his views began to radically diverge from those of Freud, who had once been his mentor. Opposing Freud’s conception of creativity as the product of purely personal forces and experiences, particularly traumatic experiences early in life and the subsequent neuroses they produce, Jung came to see such “reductive analysis” as “the most virulent poison imaginable for the attitude of the artist and the creative person in general.” Instead, he insisted that although all artistic creation draws on the personal experience of the artist — “the pregnant chaos” inside that defies “our picture of a well-ordered cosmos” — it is at bottom impersonal because its raw material is the collective unconscious:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)Great poetry draws its strength from the life of mankind, and we completely miss its meaning if we try to derive it from personal factors. Whenever the collective unconscious becomes a living experience and is brought to bear upon the conscious outlook of an age, this event is a creative act which is of importance to everyone living in that age. A work of art is produced that contains what may truthfully be called a message to generations… Every period has its bias, its particular prejudice and its psychic ailment. An epoch is like an individual; it has its own limitations of conscious outlook, and therefore requires a compensatory adjustment. This is effected by the collective unconscious in that a poet, a seer or a leader allows himself to be guided by the unexpressed desire of his times and shows the way, by word or deed, to the attainment of that which everyone blindly craves and expects — whether this attainment results in good or evil, the healing of an epoch or its destruction.

The creative person, therefore, is always living with the paradox of being a person and being an impersonal channel for the mystery beyond. Jung writes:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)Every creative person is a duality or a synthesis of contradictory aptitudes. On the one side he is a human being with a personal life, while on the other side he is an impersonal, creative process… Art is a kind of innate drive that seizes a human being and makes him its instrument. The artist is not a person endowed with free will who seeks his own ends, but one who allows art to realize its purpose through him. As a human being he may have moods and a will and personal aims, but as an artist he is [“man”](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=ac832d4b79&e=f6b8863c15) in a higher sense — he is “collective man” — one who carries and shapes the unconscious, psychic life of mankind.

[![](https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/downadownderry_dorothylathrop17.jpg)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=60640c229d&e=f6b8863c15)

Art by [Dorothy Lathrop](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=7aa08d5353&e=f6b8863c15), 1922. (Available [as a print](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=3248683cc1&e=f6b8863c15) and [as stationery cards](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=a39e44653c&e=f6b8863c15).)

Although Jung detested the myth of the tortured genius — he called the idea that suffering is essential for creation “a failure and a bungling,” “part of the general lunacy of our time” — he recognized that there is inherent suffering in the creative life itself, for the psyche is sundered by this tension between the personal and the impersonal:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)The artist’s life cannot be otherwise than full of conflicts, for two forces are at war within him — on the one hand the common human longing for happiness, satisfaction and security in life, and on the other a ruthless passion for creation which may go so far as to override every personal desire…. A person must pay dearly for the divine gift of creative fire [because] each of us [is] endowed at birth with a certain capital of energy [and] a special ability means a heavy expenditure of energy in a particular direction, with a consequent drain from some other side of life.

> […]

> Whenever the creative force predominates, human life is ruled and moulded by the unconscious as against the active will, and the conscious ego is swept along on a subterranean current, behind nothing more than a helpless observer.

From this arises a sobering antidote to the current moral fashion of renouncing works of art because the artist’s personal life has been found wanting by our current moral standards. Jung writes:

> ![](https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png)The secret of artistic creation and of the effectiveness of art is to be found in a return to… that level of experience at which it is man who lives, and not the individual, and at which the weal or woe of a single human being does not count, but only human existence. This is why every great work of art is objective and impersonal, but none the less profoundly moves us each and all. And this is also why the personal life of the poet cannot be held essential to but at most a his art — but at most a help or a hindrance to his creative task.

[![](https://i1.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2018/04/margaretcook_leavesofgrass19.jpg)](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=3f8a974ade&e=f6b8863c15)

Illustration by Margaret C. Cook for a [rare 1913 edition of *Leaves of Grass*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=3e2cb17c69&e=f6b8863c15). (Available [as a print](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=eea115fd1f&e=f6b8863c15).)

Jung adapted this chapter on creativity in [***Modern Man in Search of a Soul***](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=71a8c14a50&e=f6b8863c15) ([*public library*](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=8fb0fcd4d2&e=f6b8863c15)) from an essay he had published a decade earlier in an obscure journal that had left him feeling defeated by the signal-to-noise ratio of the social media of his time: “These days the voice of the single individual is almost completely drowned out in the chaos of newspapers and the flood of books,” he had then lamented in a letter to a colleague, not knowing that he himself would become one of the great visionaries to touch the life of humankind for epochs to come — assurance for all the half-defeated visionaries languishing in some quiet Substack, their voices muffled by the noise of now but bound to bellow into the centuries.

Couple with a glimpse inside [the creative process of great artists](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=fa7feff76d&e=f6b8863c15), then revisit Jung on [how to navigate uncertainty](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=89d8e52d17&e=f6b8863c15), Mary Oliver on [the “third self” of the creative life](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=5a21f66fcf&e=f6b8863c15), and Gary Snyder on [how to harness the creative force](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=bcbca9c131&e=f6b8863c15).

### [FROM THE ARCHIVE | Beethoven’s “Ode to Joy” Brought to Life in a Spanish Flashmob of 100 Musicians](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=8f1b502181&e=f6b8863c15)

Imagine what life would be like if lived, in May Sarton’s lovely phrase, with [“joy instead of will.”](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=d31ed563b1&e=f6b8863c15) That is what Beethoven imagined, and invited humanity to imagine, two centuries ago in the choral finale of his ninth and final symphony, known as “Ode to Joy” — an epochal hymn of the possible, [half a lifetime in the making](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=7be95d5a4e&e=f6b8863c15).

In the spring of 2012, the Spanish city of Sabadell set out to celebrate the 130th anniversary of its founding with a most unusual, electrifying, and touchingly human rendition of Beethoven’s masterpiece, performed by a flashmob of 100 musicians from the Vallès Symphony Orchestra, the Lieder, Amics de l’Òpera and Coral Belles Arts choirs. Watching the townspeople — children with kites, elders with walkers, couples holding hands — gather to savor the unbidden music in a succession of confusion, delight, and ecstasy is the stuff of goosebumps: living proof that [“music so readily transports us from the present to the past, or from what is actual to what is possible.”](https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&id=6af1c89bd7&e=f6b8863c15)

<h1 align="left" style="display:block; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:21px; font-style:normal; font-weight:bold; line-height:125%; letter-spacing:normal; border-bottom:1px solid #E19B9B; margin:0 40px; padding:0 0 7px; text-align:left; color:#262626 !important"><a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=13ba775d37&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737 !important; text-decoration:none !important">How Two Souls Can Interact with One Another: Simone de Beauvoir on Love and Friendship</a></h1>
<div style="margin:0 40px">
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left"><a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=67e2282561&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><img align="right" height="auto" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/debeauvoir\_memoir.jpg?fit=320%2C491" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; display:inline; width:150px; margin:5px 0 10px 30px" width="150"/></a></p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">It is in relationships that we discover both our depths and our limits, there that we anneal ourselves and transcend ourselves, there that we are hurt the most and there that we find the most healing.</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">But despite what a crucible of our emotional and spiritual lives relationships are — or perhaps precisely because of it — they can be riddling and nebulous, destabilizing in their fluidity and ambiguity, leaving us grasping for the comforting solidity of categories and labels. The ancient Greeks, in their pioneering effort to order the chaos of the cosmos, neatly taxonomized them into filial love (the kind we feel for siblings, children, parents, and friends), <em>eros</em> (the love of lovers), and <em>agape</em> (the deepest, purest, most impersonal and spiritual love). After the Enlightenment discounted all love as a malfunction of reason, the Romantics reclaimed it and revised the ancient taxonomy into a hierarchy, under the tyranny of which we still live, placing eros at the pinnacle of human existence. And yet our deepest relationships — the ones in which we both become most fully ourselves and are most emboldened to change — tend to <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=c9c183c81c&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">elude the commonplace classifications</a> and to <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=eeb63c9d54&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">shape-shift across the span of life</a>.</p>
<a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=e09deb73bd&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><img height="auto" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/01/simonedebeauvoir\_1946-1.jpg" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; display:inline; width:100%" width="100%"/></a><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#8C8C8C; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:0.8em; line-height:165%; text-align:left; border-bottom:1px solid #bfbfbf; padding:0 0 4px; -webkit-margin-before:0.35em">Simone de Beauvoir, 1946 (Photograph: Henri Cartier-Bresson)</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left"><a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=67fbcf2809&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">Simone de Beauvoir</a> (January 9, 1908–April 14, 1986) was only nineteen when she wielded her uncommon intellect at these questions on the pages of her journal, later published as <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=e4c709a793&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><strong><em>Diary of a Philosophy Student</em></strong></a> (<a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=0928ab233d&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><em>public library</em></a>). In between composing <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=308c593329&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">her resolutions for a life worth living</a>, Beauvoir began thinking seriously about the nature of love, its dialogue with her own nature, what she may want of it and what it may demand of her — “in brief, how souls can interact with one another.” In the midst of an intellectual infatuation with a young man who would go on to become an eminent philosopher himself — not the one she would eventually marry in a convention-breaking union of minds — she examines the substance of the feeling:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>To say that I love him, what does that mean? Does the word itself have a meaning?</p></blockquote>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Questioning the tangle of idolization and desire that masquerades as love, she grows suspicious of the very concept of personal love as an absurdity against the backdrop of the largest love we can carry:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>When you love beings… not for their intelligence, etc., but for what they have in their very depths, for their soul… you love them equally: they are entireties, perfect inasmuch as they are (to be = perfection). Why then is there this desire to get closer? To know them, and thus to love them more perfectly for what they really are. What is surprising is not that we love them all, but rather that we prefer one of them.</p></blockquote>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Invoking the love she feels for her friends, the sum total of them, she writes:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>Something sharp runs through me which is my love for them… This is not intellectual love. This is a love for souls, from all of me towards all of them in their entirety.</p></blockquote>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Over and over she returns to the elemental question:</p>

Not much, not much; I come back to this idea. Sensitivity, imagination, fatigue, and this effort to depend on another; the taste for the mystery of the other and the need to admire… What is worthwhile, is friendship… this profound mutual confidence between [two people], and this joy of knowing that the other exists.</p></blockquote>

<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Drawing on Hegel’s philosophy of freedom, in which for any conscious subject to be free means freeing the other, she arrives at a “formula” for the ideal friendship: “absolute reciprocity and the identity of consciousness.” The cultural ideal of romantic love, on the other hand, replaces this “absolute reciprocity” with engulfment and sublimation of one self into the other. She writes:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>It seems to me that love should not make all else disappear but should simply tint it with new nuances; I would like a love that accompanies me through life, not that absorbs all my life.</p></blockquote>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">This, of course, is Rilke’s <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=a6da9f481e&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">model of a perfect relationship</a> — one in which “the highest task of a bond between two people [is] that each should stand guard over the solitude of the other” — consonant with Octavio Paz’s lovely definition of love as <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=5c32372158&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">“a knot made of two intertwined freedoms.”</a></p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Beauvoir ultimately found it not in romantic love but in the deepest friendship of her life — that with Zaza, her childhood best friend.</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">A year older than her and also enamored of books, Zaza was the only one with whom the young Simone could have “real conversations.” In <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=04b08953f9&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><strong><em>Memoirs of a Dutiful Daughter</em></strong></a> (<a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=59b06dc889&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><em>public library</em></a>) — the first volume of her autobiography, largely a loving memorial to this formative relationship — she would write of talking to Zaza:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>My tongue was suddenly loosened, and a thousand bright suns began blazing in my breast; radiant with happiness.</p></blockquote>

<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>One year at a music recital [Zaza] did something while she was playing the piano which was very nearly scandalous. <p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Part of the unexamined convention Beauvoir had internalized growing up was the belief that “in a well-regulated human heart friendship occupies an honourable position, but it has neither the mysterious splendour of love, nor the sacred dignity of filial devotion.” And yet through her relationship with Zaza, she came to question this limiting “hierarchy of the emotions” and to see friendship as the deepest stratum of connection. “I loved Zaza with an intensity which could not be accounted for by any established set of rules and conventions,” she would reflect decades later.</p>

<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>I found Zaza again! <blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>I didn’t require Zaza to have any such definite feelings about me: it was enough to be her best friend. <p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Midway through Beauvoir’s sophomore year, Zaza died suddenly and mysteriously — an illness swift and merciless as an owl. She was 21. Amid the savage grief, Beauvoir turned even more sharply toward philosophy, seeking <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=2f10950cf8&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">its eternal consolations</a>. Across the sweep of the years and decades, Zaza’s inextinguishable presence never left her life. (“No one you love is ever dead,” Ernest Hemingway wrote around that time in <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=0531047049&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">a letter of consolation</a> to an inconsolable friend.) Loving Zaza had ignited Beauvoir’s becoming, setting her on the course of who she would become — one of humanity’s most daring breakers of convention, her ideas reaching into the depths of her time, shaping the times to come, touching the lives of generations of strangers the way a true friendship does. <h1 align="left" style="display:block; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:21px; font-style:normal; font-weight:bold; line-height:125%; letter-spacing:normal; border-bottom:1px solid #E19B9B; margin:0 40px; padding:0 0 7px; text-align:left; color:#262626 !important"><a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=1da5148319&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737 !important; text-decoration:none !important">Carl Jung on Creativity</a></h1>
<div style="margin:0 40px">
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left"><a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=36b70e70c3&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><img align="right" height="auto" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/jung\_soul.jpg?fit=320%2C508" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; display:inline; width:150px; margin:5px 0 10px 30px" width="150"/></a></p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">The question of what it takes to create — to make something of beauty and substance that touches other lives across space and time — is one of the deepest, oldest questions, perhaps because the answer to it is so unbearably simple: everything. We bring everything we are and everything we have lived to every smallest creative act — every experience, every dream, every memory, every unremembered impression, every unconsciously absorbed influence. The great bewilderment is that we can only access a fraction of our own everythingness — most of it dwells in the recesses of the mind and the psyche, below the level of our surface awareness. Creativity is the periscope through which the unconscious looks out onto the world and renders what it sees. The rendering is what we call art, and it is as much a picture of the seer as of the seen.</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">In the middle of the world’s most destructive war, <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=280c3c6a80&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">Carl Jung</a> took up this elemental mystery of the creative spirit in a chapter of his 1939 book <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=dbc653454b&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><strong><em>Modern Man in Search of a Soul</em></strong></a> (<a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=e18b389dc9&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><em>public library</em></a>).</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Living in that liminal epoch between the age of mysticism, when creativity was considered a divine gift superintended by muses and shamans, and the age of science, which aimed its forceps and fMRIs at regions of the brain hoping to locate the mind and microscopize the soul, Jung believed that “the human psyche is the womb of all the sciences and arts,” that the unconscious is “the necessary undercurrent of all creativity,” and that to understand how a work of art comes into being is to behold “the warp and weft of the mind in all its amazing intricacy.” Though rigorous and systematic in his approach, he was never seduced by the reductionism science often tends toward, including his own young science of psychology, once writing to a colleague:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>The creative imagination is the only primordial phenomenon accessible to us, the real Ground of the psyche, the only immediate reality… All other realities are derived from and indirectly revealed by it, actually with the artificial aid named science.</p></blockquote>
<a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=a3ae967ffd&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><img height="auto" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2012/03/carljung.jpg?w=680" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; display:inline; width:100%" width="100%"/></a><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#8C8C8C; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:0.8em; line-height:165%; text-align:left; border-bottom:1px solid #bfbfbf; padding:0 0 4px; -webkit-margin-before:0.35em">Carl Jung</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Long before psychologist Jerome Bruner itemized <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=6f427ef4c4&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">the six pillars of creativity</a> and neurologist Oliver Sacks contemplated <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=7fd97538b5&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">its three essential elements</a>, Jung foregrounds his perspective with a lucid caveat about the limitations of reason in comprehending the unconscious. In a sentiment evocative of Virginia Woolf’s astute observation that <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=df2fe5b387&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">“one can’t write directly about the soul [because] looked at, it vanishes,”</a> Jung writes:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>The creative aspect of life, which finds its clearest expression in art, baffles all attempts at rational formation. Any reaction to stimulus may be causally explained; but the creative act, which is the absolute antithesis of mere reaction, will forever elude the human understanding. It can only be described in its manifestations; it can be obscurely sensed, but never wholly grasped.</p></blockquote>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Jung approaches this acausal mystery of creativity by delineating two distinct modes of creation — the psychological and the visionary — each drawing on different aspects of existence and making different demands on us. He considers the first:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>The psychological mode deals with materials drawn from the realm of human consciousness — for instance, with the lessons of life, with emotional shocks, the experience of passion and the crises of human destiny in general — all of which go to make up the conscious life of man, and his feeling life in particular. This material is psychically assimilated by the poet, raised from the commonplace to the level of poetic experience, and given an expression which forces the reader to greater clarity and depth of human insight by bringing fully into his consciousness what <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=2bfa078087&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">he</a> ordinarily evades and overlooks or senses only with a feeling of dull discomfort. The poet’s work is an interpretation and illumination of the contents of consciousness, of the ineluctable experiences of human life with its eternally recurrent sorrow and joy. He leaves nothing over for the psychologist… Such themes go to make up the lot humankind; they repeat themselves millions of times… No obscurity whatever surrounds them, for they fully explain themselves.</p></blockquote>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Jung contrasts this with the visionary mode of creation, in which the conditions are reversed:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>[In the visionary mode] the experience that furnishes the material for artistic expression is no longer familiar. It is a strange something that derives its existence from the hinterland of man’s mind — that suggests the abyss of time separating us from pre-human ages, or evokes a super-human world of contrasting light and darkness. It is a primordial experience which surpasses man’s understanding, and to which he is therefore in danger of succumbing. The value and the force of the experience are given by its enormity. It arises form timeless depths… that in every way exceed the grasp of human feeling and comprehension [which] makes quite other demands upon the powers of the artist than do the experiences of the foreground of life. These never rend the curtain that veils the cosmos; they never transcend the bounds of the humanly possible, and for this reason are readily shaped to the demands of art, no matter how great a shock to the individual they may be. But the primordial experiences rend from top to bottom the curtain upon which is painted the picture of an ordered world, and allow a glimpse into the unfathomed abyss of what has not yet become.</p></blockquote>
<a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=847ed43930&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><img height="auto" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2014/01/thelittleprince\_morgan10.jpg?w=680" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; display:inline; width:100%" width="100%"/></a><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#8C8C8C; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:0.8em; line-height:165%; text-align:left; border-bottom:1px solid #bfbfbf; padding:0 0 4px; -webkit-margin-before:0.35em">One of Antoine de Saint-Exupéry’s <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=d646e3b110&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">original watercolors for <em>The Little Prince</em></a></p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">While the psychological mode reflects and reckons with the realities of everyday life, the visionary mode is closer to the realm of <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=b814a3bfd0&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">dreams</a>, which Jung considered “eclipses of consciousness” — those bewilderments that confuse our sense of reality and beckon interpretation, the way a great poem might, effecting “the frightening revelation of abysses that defy the human understanding.” He writes:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0">
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>In dealing with the psychological mode of artistic creation, we never need ask ourselves what the material consists of or what it means. But this question forces itself upon us as soon as we come to the visionary mode of creation. We are astonished, taken aback, confused, put on our guard or even disgusted — and we demand commentaries and explanations. We are reminded in nothing of everyday, human life, but rather of dreams, nighttime fears and dark recesses of the mind that we sometimes sense with misgiving.</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left">[…]</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left">A great work of art is like a dream; for all its apparent obviousness it does not explain itself and is never unequivocal. A dream never says: “You ought,” or: “This is the truth.” It presents an image in much the same way as nature allows a plant to grow, and we must draw our own conclusions.</p>
</blockquote>
<a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=9151e2a54b&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><img height="auto" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/02/davidthedreamer10.jpg?w=680" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; display:inline; width:100%" width="100%"/></a><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#8C8C8C; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:0.8em; line-height:165%; text-align:left; border-bottom:1px solid #bfbfbf; padding:0 0 4px; -webkit-margin-before:0.35em">Illustration by Tom Seidmann-Freud from <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=2a4f3b32ec&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><em>David the Dreamer: His Book of Dreams</em></a>, 1922.</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">The psychological mode of creation concerns itself with an overt rendering of human emotion as we know it and experience it, but the visionary reaches beyond <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=9058550bf7&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">the horizon of our self-knowledge</a> and into those depths that only the tendrils of <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=4362ada6ee&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">our intuitions</a> every touch. Because what we find there may so alarm us, may so contradict our conscious self-image, we tend to doubt our discoveries and retreat behind “the armor of reason” to dismiss them. Jung writes:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>Human passion falls within the sphere of conscious experience, while the subject of the vision lies beyond it. Through our feelings we experience the known, but our intuitions point to things that are unknown and hidden — that by their very nature are secret. If ever they become conscious, they are intentionally kept back and concealed, for which reason they have been regarded from earlier times as mysterious, uncanny, and deceptive.</p></blockquote>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Holding up the poet in that Baldwinian sense (“The poets (by which I mean all artists),” Baldwin <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=4f41f6ff73&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">wrote</a>, “are finally the only people who know the truth about us. Soldiers don’t. Statesmen don’t… Only poets.”), Jung adds:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>In our midst, the poet now and then catches sight of the figures that people the night-world — the spirits, demons and gods. He knows that a purposiveness out-reaching human ends is the life-giving secret for man; he has a presentiment of incomprehensible happenings in the pleroma.</p></blockquote>
<a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=4890d41edd&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><img height="auto" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2025/04/BarredOwl\_margin.jpg" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; display:inline; width:100%" width="100%"/></a><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#8C8C8C; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:0.8em; line-height:165%; text-align:left; border-bottom:1px solid #bfbfbf; padding:0 0 4px; -webkit-margin-before:0.35em">Art from <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=ea20ed0976&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><em>An Almanac of Birds: 100 Divinations for Uncertain Days</em></a>, also available as a <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=f5df9b207d&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">stand-alone print</a>.</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">It is not only great artists, he observes, who can access those hidden places but also “the seers, prophets, leaders and enlighteners.” A year after science split the atom and a century before it began intimating that <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=36d4a0a173&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">consciousness may be as old as the universe and fundamental to it</a>, Jung writes:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>Is there something more purposeful than electrons? Do we delude ourselves in thinking that we possess and command our own souls? And is that which science calls the “psyche” not merely a question-mark arbitrarily confined within the skull, but rather a door that opens upon the human world from a world beyond, now and again allowing strange and unseizable potencies to act upon man and to remove him, as if upon the wings of the night, from the level of common humanity to that of a more than personal vocation?</p></blockquote>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">That more-than-personal aspect of visionary work is what Jung calls “the collective unconscious.” It is where his views began to radically diverge from those of Freud, who had once been his mentor. Opposing Freud’s conception of creativity as the product of purely personal forces and experiences, particularly traumatic experiences early in life and the subsequent neuroses they produce, Jung came to see such “reductive analysis” as “the most virulent poison imaginable for the attitude of the artist and the creative person in general.” Instead, he insisted that although all artistic creation draws on the personal experience of the artist — “the pregnant chaos” inside that defies “our picture of a well-ordered cosmos” — it is at bottom impersonal because its raw material is the collective unconscious:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>Great poetry draws its strength from the life of mankind, and we completely miss its meaning if we try to derive it from personal factors. Whenever the collective unconscious becomes a living experience and is brought to bear upon the conscious outlook of an age, this event is a creative act which is of importance to everyone living in that age. A work of art is produced that contains what may truthfully be called a message to generations… Every period has its bias, its particular prejudice and its psychic ailment. An epoch is like an individual; it has its own limitations of conscious outlook, and therefore requires a compensatory adjustment. This is effected by the collective unconscious in that a poet, a seer or a leader allows himself to be guided by the unexpressed desire of his times and shows the way, by word or deed, to the attainment of that which everyone blindly craves and expects — whether this attainment results in good or evil, the healing of an epoch or its destruction.</p></blockquote>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">The creative person, therefore, is always living with the paradox of being a person and being an impersonal channel for the mystery beyond. Jung writes:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>Every creative person is a duality or a synthesis of contradictory aptitudes. On the one side he is a human being with a personal life, while on the other side he is an impersonal, creative process… Art is a kind of innate drive that seizes a human being and makes him its instrument. The artist is not a person endowed with free will who seeks his own ends, but one who allows art to realize its purpose through him. As a human being he may have moods and a will and personal aims, but as an artist he is <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=ac832d4b79&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">“man”</a> in a higher sense — he is “collective man” — one who carries and shapes the unconscious, psychic life of mankind.</p></blockquote>
<a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=60640c229d&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline"><img height="auto" src="https://i0.wp.com/www.themarginalian.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/03/downadownderry\_dorothylathrop17.jpg" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; display:inline; width:100%" width="100%"/></a><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#8C8C8C; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:0.8em; line-height:165%; text-align:left; border-bottom:1px solid #bfbfbf; padding:0 0 4px; -webkit-margin-before:0.35em">Art by <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=7aa08d5353&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">Dorothy Lathrop</a>, 1922. (Available <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=3248683cc1&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">as a print</a> and <a href="https://themarginalian.us2.list-manage.com/track/click?u=13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1&amp;id=a39e44653c&amp;e=f6b8863c15" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#C33737; text-decoration:underline">as stationery cards</a>.)</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">Although Jung detested the myth of the tortured genius — he called the idea that suffering is essential for creation “a failure and a bungling,” “part of the general lunacy of our time” — he recognized that there is inherent suffering in the creative life itself, for the psyche is sundered by this tension between the personal and the impersonal:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0">
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>The artist’s life cannot be otherwise than full of conflicts, for two forces are at war within him — on the one hand the common human longing for happiness, satisfaction and security in life, and on the other a ruthless passion for creation which may go so far as to override every personal desire…. A person must pay dearly for the divine gift of creative fire [because] each of us [is] endowed at birth with a certain capital of energy [and] a special ability means a heavy expenditure of energy in a particular direction, with a consequent drain from some other side of life.</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left">[…]</p>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left">Whenever the creative force predominates, human life is ruled and moulded by the unconscious as against the active will, and the conscious ego is swept along on a subterranean current, behind nothing more than a helpless observer.</p>
</blockquote>
<p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#262626; font-family:Georgia, serif; font-size:16px; line-height:165%; text-align:left">From this arises a sobering antidote to the current moral fashion of renouncing works of art because the artist’s personal life has been found wanting by our current moral standards. Jung writes:</p>
<blockquote style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; display:block; -webkit-margin-before:0; -webkit-margin-after:0; -webkit-margin-start:40px; -webkit-margin-end:0"><p align="left" style="-webkit-text-size-adjust:100%; -ms-text-size-adjust:100%; color:#595959; font-family:Arial, Sans-serif; font-size:15px; line-height:170%; text-align:left"><img align="left" height="auto" src="https://gallery.mailchimp.com/13eb080d8a315477042e0d5b1/images/2e292385-dc1c-4cfe-b95e-845f6f98c2ec.png" style="-ms-interpolation-mode:bicubic; border:0; height:auto; line-height:100%; outline:none; text-decoration:none; padding:5px 30px 10px 0; display:inline; width:50px !important" width="50px !important"/>The secret of artistic creation and of the effectiveness of art is to be found in a return to… that level of experience at which it is man who lives, and not the individual, and at which the weal or woe of a single human being does not count, but only human existence.
