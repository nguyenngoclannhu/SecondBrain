## Full Document
The year I sold my restaurants, I found out I had no idea what silence sounded like.

A working kitchen is a wall of noise. Extraction fans, tickets printing, someone bellowing about the sea bass. Years of that, and then one morning it was a flat, a quiet street and the sound of my own feet on the floor. I lasted four days before I started putting something on. A podcast in the shower. A playlist while I cooked. The telly murmuring in the next room while I pretended to read.

I told myself I liked background noise, which was true. It was not the whole truth.

#### Nobody much wants to be alone in a room with themselves

In 2014, a team led by Timothy Wilson at the University of Virginia ran [eleven studies](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4330241) in which people sat in a bare room for six to fifteen minutes with nothing to do but think. Most did not enjoy it, and rated mundane chores as far more pleasant. In one version, participants who had already said they would pay money to avoid a mild electric shock chose to shock themselves rather than sit there thinking. Two thirds of the men. A quarter of the women.

The finding went everywhere, and it earned the pushback it got. Other researchers went back through the numbers and [argued the interpretation was overcooked](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01427/full), noting that people rated the experience around the middle of the scale rather than hating it outright. Fair enough. Mild boredom on a plastic chair is not proof of inner torment.

Even so, something in it landed for me. Handed a genuinely empty ten minutes, most of us go looking for a switch.

#### Your favourite host is doing a job you never offered them

Here is the part I find quietly astonishing. Psychologists have a name for what your headphones are up to: [social surrogacy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0022103108002412). Across four studies, researchers found people reported turning to favoured television programmes when they felt lonely, and felt less lonely while watching. Merely thinking about a favourite show buffered against the dip in mood and self esteem that normally follows a row with someone close.

Note the word favoured. Not whatever happens to be on. Shows with people in them you feel you know.

Audio pulls the same trick with fewer moving parts. A study in [PLOS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0265806) found that listeners who developed one sided bonds with their hosts reported a stronger sense of relatedness, which is the technical term for feeling connected to other people. The same paper found that raw listening hours predicted almost nothing on their own. The bond did the work.

Which explains something I could never account for. There are three people whose voices I have heard more often in the last five years than my own brother’s. They have no idea I exist. My brain has not fully accepted this.

#### Loneliness rarely turns up wearing a name tag

John Cacioppo spent a career arguing that loneliness functions like [hunger or thirst](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3855545): an unpleasant signal with a job to do. It is meant to be uncomfortable, because the discomfort is what gets you up and out to find your people. Hunger says eat. Loneliness says go and see someone.

The problem with a signal is that muting it feels almost identical to answering it. A warm familiar voice in your ear registers as company. The ache goes quiet. Nothing about your actual life has changed, but the alarm has stopped ringing, so you assume the fire is out.

That is why almost nobody who does this would describe themselves as lonely. They would say they are busy, or that they hate silence, or that they are just really into true crime. All of which can be true while something else goes on underneath.

#### The bill arrives quietly

The researchers behind the surrogacy work were careful about this, and their caution is the most useful thing in the literature. One of them [noted](https://www.buffalo.edu/news/releases/2009/04/10063.html) that for people cut off by illness or circumstance, borrowed belonging can be a genuine comfort. But turning away from real friends and family for the solace of a screen may leave someone with fewer resources as the years go on.

That is the trap. The surrogate works, and because it works you never make the slightly awkward phone call, or accept the invitation you are too tired for, or sit with the discomfort long enough to hear what it is asking of you.

#### A test worth running this week

You do not have to become one of those people who walks around in reverent silence. I am not throwing out my headphones. But try these.

Pick one recurring slot and strip it back. The commute, the washing up, the twenty minutes before bed. One, not all of them. Give it a week before you judge it, because the first few days are unpleasant and that is rather the point.

Notice when you reach for the switch. Not to stop yourself, just to clock it. Mine was always the moment I walked through the door to an empty flat. That timing told me more than any amount of introspection.

Then do the annoying bit. When the ache shows up, treat it as an instruction rather than a mood. Text the friend. Book the coffee. Go and eat somewhere with other humans in it and nothing in your ears.

I still put podcasts on in the shower. But I stopped filling every gap out of reflex, and six months later I noticed I was seeing people more, which I had never consciously decided to do. The noise had been answering a question I was not letting myself hear.
