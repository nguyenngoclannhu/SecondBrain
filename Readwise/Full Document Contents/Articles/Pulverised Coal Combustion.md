Pulverized [coal combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/coal-combustion) (PCC) is the most widely used technology for coal power generation. In this process, coal is milled, pulverized, and injected in the boiler with air to allow combustion. Combustion takes place at temperatures from 1300 to 1700 °C, depending largely on coal rank [17]. The heat generated is used to produce a steam of high pressure (25–30 bars) and temperature to power high and medium turbines that are connected to a generator which produces electric energy (Fig. 1).

Pulverized coal combustion leads to the generation of coal combustion by-products, mostly ashes. The coarser fraction of ashes, bottom ash or slag, is removed from the bottom of the boiler, whereas the finest particles, or fly ashes, are retained from the [flue gas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/flue-gas) stream by the particulate controls, usually [Electrostatic Precipitators](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/electrostatic-precipitator) (ESP) or Fabric Filters (FF). Despite the high efficiency of the ESPs and FFs (>99%) [18,19], a small fraction of fly ashes escapes from the controls and reaches the [FGD](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/flue-gas) system (Fig. 1).

The conventional process of coal combustion can be modified to improve the energetic efficiency of the process and reduce the environmental impacts. The *[Fluidized Bed Combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/fluidized-bed-combustion)* (FBC) allows a better use of the fuel and a better transference of the heat for power generation. The basis for a [FBC system](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/fluidized-bed-combustion-system) and the main difference with respect to the conventional PCC is the formation of a bed of an inert material, such as sand or limestone, through which air is blown from below at sufficient velocity to suspend particles. The melting of the bed is not reached by limiting [combustion temperature](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/combustion-temperature) to 850–950 °C. Due to the lower temperatures and the longer residence time with respect to PCC, the FBC produces less NOx emissions and reduces the SO2 emissions when limestone is added to coal or to the [fluidized bed](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/fluidized-bed) [18]. There are three different types of FBC available: the *[Circulating FBC](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/circulating-fluidized-bed-combustion)* (CFBC)**,** the *[Bubbling FBC](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/bubbling-fluidized-bed)* (BFBC), and *Pressurized FBC* (PFBC) [23].

Another option for coal power generation is *co-combustion* or *co-firing* which entails the simultaneous combustion of two or more fuels in the same power plant in order to produce one or more energy carriers [20]. The worldwide electricity demand and the environmental regulations led to a market increase of the use of the co-combustion matters such as [petroleum coke](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/petroleum-coke), [sewage sludge](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/sewage-sludge), and biomass in coal power generation.

*Oxy-fuel combustion* is a promising technology that may greatly contribute to the reduction of carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from power plants. The principle of oxy-fuel combustion is to increase the [partial pressure](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/partial-pressure) of CO2 in the flue gas in order to make its sequestration and compression easier and more cost effective [21]. This can be achieved by burning a fuel with pure oxygen instead of air as a primary oxidant, which results in higher flame temperatures. For this reason, the mixture is diluted with a portion of the resulting flue gas composed primarily of CO2. Fundamentally, due to the variation in the oxidant, and consequently in the in-furnace gas environment (as compared to conventional air-fired combustion), oxy-fuel combustion affects the [combustion process](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion) of [pulverized coal](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverised-coal) as well as related processes such as heat transfer [22]. As a result, most studies and research projects have focused on fundamental scientific and engineering issues (e.g. ignition, flame stability, heat transfer, [recycled flue gas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/recycled-flue-gas) ratio, and combustion characteristics).

##### 3.1 Pulverized Coal Combustion

[Pulverized coal](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverised-coal) (PC) combustion is presently the system of choice for coal-fired power-generating plants. In [PC](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverised-coal) combustion, the coal is dried and is ground to a specified fineness, with a maximum particle size of 250–300 μm, depending on the reactivity of the coal. Coal preparation, which involves feeding, drying, and [grinding](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/grinding-machining) of the coal, and the [pneumatic transport](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pneumatic-transport) of the [pulverized coal](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverised-coal) to the burners, is fully integrated with the boiler. For lower [reactivity coals](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/coal-reactivity), the fineness of grind is increased to create a larger specific surface area so as to improve conditions for ignition and combustion. The powdered coal is pneumatically transported to burners and injected via particle-laden jets into the [combustion chamber](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion-chamber). The transport air that carries the coal from the mill to the burners is a small fraction of the total [combustion air](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion-air). It is kept at low temperature, limited to about 373 K, for reasons of safety against ignition and explosion in the mill and in the powdered coal transport pipeline between the mill and the burners. The rest of the combustion air, which can be preheated to higher temperatures, is injected separately and admixed with the already ignited coal particle-laden jet in the combustion chamber. A schematic illustration of a PC combustion boiler is shown in Fig. 3. The combustion chamber is typically of [parallelepiped](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/parallelepiped) shape; the dimensions of a 300-MW coal-fired boiler would be approximately be 15×15 m2 of cross-sectional area and 45–50 m in height. The combustion chamber walls are completely cooled by steam-generating tubes. As the pulverized coal particles burn, the flame transfers heat, mainly by [thermal radiation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/thermal-radiation), to the steam-cooled tube walls of the boiler. The design of the combustion chamber has to provide for sufficient residence time of the burning particle to [complete combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/complete-combustion), and for the cooling of the residual fly ash particles to below their “softening temperature” before entry to narrow convective heat exchanger passages, to prevent the buildup of sticky ash deposits. At regular intervals, friable deposits are removed by [soot blowing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/soot-blowing) using steam.

Figure 3. Utility boiler fired with pulverized coal.

Although there are a great variety of burners in PC combustion boilers, the most widespread types are the circular burners and the vertical nozzle arrays. Circular burners are usually positioned perpendicularly to the combustion chamber walls, whereas vertical nozzle arrays are in the corners, firing tangentially onto the circumference of an imaginary cylinder in the middle of the combustion chamber. Designs utilizing circular burners are more concerned with the tailoring of the individual burner flame, whereas vertical nozzle arrays in tangentially [fired furnaces](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/fired-furnace) are designed to rely more on the furnace volume for the mixing of the fuel and air streams. Complete combustion with a minimum (typically less than 15%) of excess air is a challenging task for a [coal combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/coal-combustion) system. The excess air, usually expressed as the percentage of the theoretically required combustion air, is the additional air needed for complete burnout of the fuel. The significance of the excess air is that the heat carried by the stack gases into the atmosphere, the largest heat loss (about 10%) of boiler operation, is directly proportional to the volume of the [flue gas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/flue-gas) and, hence, to the excess air.

The fly ash formed in pulverized coal combustion is removed from the flue gas in the form of dry [particulate matter](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/atmospheric-aerosol); a small proportion (∼10%) of the coal ash that falls off the tube walls as semimolten agglomerated ash is collected from the bottom hopper of the combustion chamber (“bottom ash”). Ash particles are molten in the high-temperature regions of the flame, but cool and solidify as they reach heat exchange surfaces of the boiler, forming friable deposits that are easily removed by soot blowing.

In pulverized [coal combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/coal-combustion) (PCC), dust burners are used to feed coal into the [combustion chamber](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion-chamber), where flame core zones of the combustion chamber having a temperature range between 1450°C and 1600°C are present. To attain the required efficiency, pulverized coal is given a swirling motion around the flame core. The small quantity of coal does not pass through the flame core zone and hence does not undergo [complete combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/complete-combustion). Although the temperature of the furnace is greater than the melting point of some of the materials, approximately 20%–50% of the materials are left nonmelted. The process of combustion in [PCC](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/coal-combustion) is a [multistage](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/multistage) process. In the first step at temperatures around 350°C, there is a release of the volatile components due to the evaporation of water, which is physically absorbed on the surface of fuel particles. In the next step, between the temperature of approximately 350°C–600°C, softening of coal grains occurs that accelerates and further liberates volatile components. The dehydroxylation process is subjected to loamy materials within this temperature range. In the final step, for temperatures above 600°C, there is a thermal swell of coal grains leading to absolute [devolatilization](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/devolatilization) and hence resulting in coke formation due to [autoignition](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/autoignition). The total duration of this process is about 0.01–0.2 s. Further, the combustion of coke residues takes place, and disintegration of coal grains because of [oxidation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/oxidation-reaction) of fuel as well as existing mineral matters occurs. The duration of this event is 0.5–5 s. The step leads to the formation of thick and fine fractions of slag and fly ash particles, respectively. Mineral matter, which is hugely diffused is melted and gets transformed into a spherical shape. A large number of [microspheres](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/microsphere) and spheres that are formed swell due to the degassing process. Slag, which is a by-product of combustion, gets accumulated at the bottom of the combustion chamber under the grate, while fly ash, also a by-product of coal combustion, is separated from [flue gases](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/flue-gas) with the help of fans in the combustion chamber (Solomon et al., 1993).

[![Handbook of Fly Ash](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/3-s2.0-C20180016552-cov200h.gif)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/book/9780128176863)[Handbook of Fly Ash](https://www.sciencedirect.com/book/9780128176863)
Coal-based power plants with conventional stoke firing suffer from some shortcomings such as the inability to handle load fluctuations due to limited combustion capacity, difficulties in removing large quantities of ash, and interference of the formed ash in the [combustion process](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion). Pulverized [coal combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/coal-combustion) systems offer a viable solution to all these problems, wherein coal is reduced to a [fine powder](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/finer-powder) in grinding mills and projected into the [combustion chamber](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/combustion-chamber) through a hot primary air current. To ensure [complete combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/complete-combustion), supplementary air (known as secondary air) is delivered separately to the combustion chamber. Globally, almost 97% of the coal power plants with sizes up to 1000 MW are run by [pulverized coal](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverised-coal). The efficiency of the [pulverized fuel firing](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverized-fuel-boiler) system mostly depends on the size of the powder, and average net efficiencies reach up to 35% (Nicol, 2013). Pulverized coal technology is classified (Table 2.1) based on the operating temperature and pressure, wherein subcritical plants operate below the [critical point](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/critical-point) of water, and the supercritical, ultrasupercritical, and advanced ultrasupercritical (AUSC) work beyond the critical point. The efficiency of these technologies increases (from <35% for subcritical to 52% for AUSC) with their operating temperatures and pressures.

Pulverized fuel combustion is the dominant technology to burn solid fuels because of its high combustion intensity (0.5–1.0 MW m− 3) and high [heat transfer rates](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/heat-transfer-rate) (0.1–1.0 MW m− 2), which leads to low-cost plants [19–21]. Pulverized [coal combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/coal-combustion) is a well-established technology that accounts for almost 97% of the world's coal-fired [power generation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/power-generation) capacity [4]. Those units require the reduction of large size coal particles to a powdered form and carry the particles, using a carrier gas, into the [combustion chamber](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion-chamber) (i.e., furnaces and boilers). Even though several advantages for [pulverized fuel](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverized-fuel) combustion, significant challenges remain, mainly in limiting pollutant (e.g., NOx, CO2, and SO2) emissions and increasing plant efficiency.

The most common type of coal-fired power plant is [pulverized coal](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverised-coal) combustion (PCC), shown in Figure 2.1. A mixture of pulverized coal and air is blown into a low NOx burner. This burner has an annular arrangement. Coal and a portion of the air are fed to the center tube. The remainder of the air is fed through the space between the inside and outside tubes. The main portion of the flame has a low oxygen/fuel ratio and a relatively low temperature, both of which inhibit formation of NOx. The additional air oxidizes CO to CO2. A low NOx burner reduces NOx emissions from about 11 kg to about 5.5 kg per ton of sub-bituminous coal.2

![](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/3-s2.0-B9780815520498100026-f02-01-9780815520498.jpg)Figure 2.1. Pulverized coal combustion plant.
The walls of the furnace have a water wall construction, meaning that side-by-side tubes are welded together to form a continuous wall. Hot [combustion gas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion-gas) first rises through the boiler section where pressurized water is boiled to make steam. Next comes the [superheater](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/superheater) section, where the steam temperature is raised above its [boiling point](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/boiling-point). Then the [economizer](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/economizer) section preheats the [boiler feed water](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/boiler-feedwater). Finally there is a rotating plate exchanger. Iron plates rotate into the path of the warm [flue gas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/flue-gas). The warm plates then rotate out of the flue gas path and into the air path, where the plates preheat [combustion air](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion-air).

[Flue gas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/flue-gas) then enters the [selective catalytic reactor](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/selective-catalytic-reactor) (SCR). Ammonia is injected into the SCR where it reacts with NOx (here shown as NO) to form N2 and H2O. This eliminates 75 to 85% of the NOx.

The flue gas then enters the bag-house, which removes fly ash. The flue gas is forced to flow through a bag filter that captures the fly ash. Some power plants use an [electrostatic precipitator](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/electrostatic-precipitator) in place of a bag-house. In this process the flue [gas flows](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/gas-flow) between two electrified parallel plates. These plates attract the fly ash to the surface of the plates where it is held through the electro-static force. The de-ashed flue gas flows on through the plates.

A [flue gas desulfurization](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/flue-gas-desulfurisation) unit (FGD) uses a wet or dry limestone (CaCO3) stream to convert SO2 to gypsum (CaSO4·2H2O), which is land-filled.

At the time this was written, there was no standard method for [mercury control](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/mercury-control). Control techniques under consideration include [flotation](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/flotation) of the coal to remove mineral matter and injection of activated carbon into the flue gas ahead of the bag-house. Mercury in the flue gas can be in either oxidized or un-oxidized form. Halide salts have been used to converted un-oxidized mercury to mercuric halides, which are more readily removed in the bag-house or FGD. The Western Research Institute has also patented a process where mercury is removed by preheating the coal to a particular temperature prior to combustion.

[![Coal Gasification and Its Applications](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/3-s2.0-C20090200675-cov200h.gif)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/book/9780815520498)[Coal Gasification and Its Applications](https://www.sciencedirect.com/book/9780815520498)
PCC technology is a widely utilized technology to generate energy from fossil fuel, especially coal [15]. In this technology, [pulverized coal](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverised-coal) is injected to combust in a furnace in the presence of a controlled level of air. The heat generated is used to produce high-pressure steam driving a [steam turbine](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/steam-turbine) to generate electrical power. The average efficiency for such plants is about 36% in the OECD (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development) countries and 30% in China [16]. The concept of PCC has been enhanced to operate at higher temperatures and pressures to produce supercritical (SC) steam and also ultra-supercritical (USC) steam (>374 °C and 218 atm). These two advanced technologies have efficiencies far greater than PCC, with efficiency ranging between 40% and 55%. However, the full commercialization of SC technology has been limited by the need for materials that can withstand high temperatures and pressures. These technologies are seen as a major carbon mitigation route, as it is estimated that a percentage point increase in plant [thermal efficiency](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/thermodynamic-efficiency) can lead to a double reduction in [CO](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/agricultural-and-biological-sciences/carbon-monoxide)2 emissions [17]. Therefore, replacing old [pulverized fuel](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/pulverized-fuel) (PF) plants with SC pulverized coal plants has the potential of reducing emissions by 10–25% [15].

##### 4.2 Combustion Processes and Technology

Coal may be burned by passing air through a bed of relatively large coal particles (solid coal combustion) or as a stream of [finely ground](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/finely-ground) (or pulverized) coal blown with air into the furnace system (pulverized coal combustion). Solid coal combustion may be used in different types of small-scale, stoker-based installations, such as [industrial boilers](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/industrial-boiler) (Cudmore, 1984, and Juniper, 2000). Pulverized coal combustion (PCC), however, is the method of choice for large-scale power generation, accounting for over 90% of the world's current electric utility capacity (International Energy Agency, 2006). Other types of systems, such as cyclone burners, are also in widespread use. Yet another technology, fluidized-bed combustion (FBC), is becoming increasingly popular in some areas (Juniper, 2000) because of its ability to handle a wide variety of low-quality fuels (including coal preparation wastes) and its capacity to produce minimal SO2 emissions from combustion of coals with relatively high [sulphur contents](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/sulphur-content).

Pulverized coal combustion (PCC) involves [grinding](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/grinding-machining) the feed coal to about 70% <70 μm and injecting the powdered coal into the boiler from either wall-mounted burners or corner-mounted (tangential) burners. Combustion takes place within a few seconds at flame temperatures up to 1,500°C. Supercritical [PCC](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/coal-combustion) is a variation that seeks to improve [thermal efficiency](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/thermodynamic-efficiency), from the typical values of up to about 40% for PCC to 43–47% in [supercritical systems](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/supercritical-system) through higher steam temperatures and pressures. For example, the Tachibanawan unit in [Japan](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/japan) operates with steam at up to 600°C and 25.1 MPa. Higher temperatures, up to 700°C, are planned but depend on the development of new materials for pipes and turbines. Such steam temperatures will raise the thermal efficiency of PCC systems to 55%. The developments are limited by engineering problems rather than coal [quality factors](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/q-factor), and the same high quality coals used for PCC will be sought for supercritical and ultra-supercritical PCC installations (summaries after Berkowitz, 1979, and Henderson, 2003).

Fluidized bed combustion (FBC) develops when a bed of fine particles of coal is subjected to an upward [gas flow](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/gas-flow). The bed remains static, but the pressure drop across it increases in proportion to the [gas flow rate](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/gas-flowrate). When the pressure drop across the [bed particles](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/fluidized-bed) equals the weight per unit area of the bed, the bed is suspended and it is at its minimum degree of [fluidization](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/fluidization). When the gas flow increases above the minimum, bubbles are produced. The movement provides intense agitation and mixing among the particles in the [fluidized bed](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/fluidized-bed). This results in the bed particles conveying the heat produced from coal combustion at very high rates to the cooler surroundings. Coal is burned in the bubbling bed at temperatures of 800–900°C, significantly lower than the temperatures in [pulverized fuel](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverized-fuel) installations. Limestone may be added to the bed, both as a support for the coal during combustion and as a [sorbent](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/sorbent) for the SO2 and other sulfur [oxide](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/oxide-compound) (SOx) emissions. In addition to the primary air in the [combustor](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/combustion-chamber), secondary air is introduced at several levels above the fluidized bed. Together with the relatively low [combustion temperatures](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/combustion-temperature), this helps reduce the levels of nitrogen [oxides](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/oxide-compound) (NOx) emitted from the system. The lower combustion temperatures also allow the use of coals with relatively low [ash fusion temperatures](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/ash-fusion-temperature).

Because of the low SOx and NOx emissions, FBC is a technology that is able to burn a variety of otherwise poor quality fuels efficiently and in an environmentally friendly way. Two main processes are in use: [bubbling FBC](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/bubbling-fluidized-bed) (BFBC) and [circulating FBC](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/circulating-fluidized-bed-combustion) (CFBC). Both may operate under atmospheric or pressurized conditions. Bubbling FBC works well with coals that have high moisture and ash percentages and with [high rank coals](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/high-rank-coal) having low proportions of volatile matter.

In CFBC the velocity of the air is increased and the fuel particles are carried upward from the bed surface. The [combustion chamber](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion-chamber) is filled with a turbulent cloud of particles that are not in close contact with each other. The burning particles are recovered from the air flow and brought back into the lower part of the combustion chamber. A [circulating fluidized bed](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/circulating-fluidized-bed) is able to sustain combustion, and the contact between coal particles stabilizes the overall temperature. CFBC involves using a coarser coal than PCC, typically 3–6 mm in diameter, in a fluidized bed of coal, ash, limestone, and air. Combustion is also at lower temperatures than for PCC (i.e., less than 1,000°C). Subcritical operation yields efficiencies of up to 40% for units up to 300 MW. Emissions are generally lower than in PCC, and thus fluidized bed units have lower levels of primary pollutants such as NOx, SO2, CO2, and [particulates](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/atmospheric-aerosol).

PFBC, operating at 1–1.5 MPa with combustion temperatures of 800–900°C, has the advantage that the hot [combustion gases](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/combustion-gas) are removed from the combustor under pressure. If these conditions are maintained and the gases are cleaned, they can be sent directly into a [gas turbine](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/gas-turbine) (Thomas, 2002). PCBC has been used commercially to burn low quality coals (Department of Trade and Industry (DTI), 2000a) but is still at the pilot stage. Both CFBC and PFBC technologies hold the promise of increased thermal efficiency, with the ability to burn low quality and even waste coal materials (summaries after Henderson, 2003, and International Energy Agency, 2006).

A further development is the use of [integrated gasification combined cycle](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/integrated-gasification-combined-cycle) (IGCC) technology (Collot, 2006, and references therein). IGCC systems produce electricity by first gasifying coal with a controlled shortage of air or oxygen in a pressurized reactor (gasifier) to generate [syngas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/synthesis-gas) (a mixture of hydrogen and carbon monoxide) that is then cooled and cleaned of impurities. The syngas is combusted with air or oxygen to drive a gas turbine. The exhaust gases are then heat-exchanged with water/steam to produce a [superheated steam](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/superheated-steam) that drives a [steam turbine](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/steam-turbine) (World Coal Institute, 2006b). This system offers efficiencies up to 50%, with a potential of 56% in the future, improving the environmental performance of coal. [Pollutant emissions](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/earth-and-planetary-sciences/pollutant-emission) are significantly reduced and up to 90% of mercury emissions can be captured (World Coal Institute, 2006b). In addition to the potential for greater thermal efficiencies, IGCC also produces a CO2 stream more amenable to [geological sequestration](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/geological-sequestration). The gasification process is not in itself a coal combustion technology and therefore is not discussed further in this chapter. However, a more detailed discussion is provided in the coal gasification section (see Chapter 5).

In the [pulverized coal](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverised-coal) combustion system, coal ground to a very fine size (70–80% passing through a 200-mesh screen or 75 μm) is blown into a furnace. Approximately about 10% of the total air required for combustion is used to transport the coal. This primary air, preheated in the [airheater](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/airheaters), is used to drive out the moisture in the coal and transport the coal to the furnace. A majority of the [combustion air](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion-air) is admitted into the furnace as secondary air, which is also preheated in the air [preheater](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/preheater). The particles are heated at 105–106 °C/sec, and it takes about 1–1.5 sec for [complete combustion](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/complete-combustion). This increases the throughput to furnace and, hence, it is the most preferred form of combustion method by electric utilities. Based on the burner configuration, pulverized coal combustion systems can be divided into wall-fired, tangentially fired, and down-fired systems. In wall-fired units the burners are mounted on a single wall or two opposite walls. Wall-fired systems with single wall burners are easy to design. However, due to uneven heat distribution, the flame stability and combustion efficiency are poor compared to the opposed wall-fired system. Because of the possibility of flame impingement on the opposite wall, this system may lead to ash slagging problems. An opposite wall-fired boiler avoids most of these problems by providing even heat distribution and better flame stability. A diagram of the opposed firing system is shown inFig. 5. However, the [heat release rate](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/heat-release-rate) can be higher than single wall burner systems and could lead to slagging and fouling problems. A tangential firing system has burners installed in all four corners of the boiler (as shown inFig. 6). The coal and primary air jets are issued at an angle, which is tangent to an imaginary circle at the center of the furnace. The four jets from four corners create a “fireball” at the center. A top view of the furnace is provided inFig. 6. The swirling flame then travels up to the furnace outlet. This method provides the highest even heating flame stability. The temperature in the “fireball” can reach as high as 1700–1800 °C. The burner is a key component in the design of the combustion system. It also determines the flow and mixing patterns, ignition of volatiles, flame stability, temperature, and generation of pollutants. Burners can be classified into two types—swirl and nonswirl. Primary air transports coal at velocities exceeding 15 meters/sec (the flame velocity) of the fuel. The [flame velocity](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/flame-velocity) is a function of volatile matter. Secondary air ignites the fuel, determines the mixing pattern, and also determines the NOx formation. High volatile [bituminous coals](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/bituminous-coal) tend to have higher flame velocities compared to [low volatile coals](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/low-volatile-coal). [Swirling flows](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/swirling-flow) promote mixing and esatablish [recirculation zones](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/recirculation-zone). In swirling flows, the axial flux of [angular momentum](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/angular-momentum) G and the axial flux of axial momentum Gx are conserved.

##### II.19.5 Hot-corrosion risk in pressurised pulverised coal combustion

Pressurised [pulverised coal](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/pulverised-coal) combustion (PPCC) is another coal-fired combined cycle concept which is able to achieve efficiencies in excess of 50% [97Han]. A schematic flow diagram is given in Fig II.19.4. Combustion of pulverised coal takes place at temperatures of about 1600 °C under a total pressure of about 15 bar. The produced [flue gas](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/flue-gas) is routed through a column of ceramic balls as a liquid-slag separation unit at an average temperature of 1450 °C. A separate alkali removal (*T* ≤ 1400 °C) is the last clean-up step before the flue gas enters the [gas turbine](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/gas-turbine). Here too, the residual [thermal energy](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/thermal-energy) of the [gas stream](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/gas-streams) leaving the turbine is finally transferred to a steam cycle.

![](https://ars.els-cdn.com/content/image/3-s2.0-B978184569215550022X-f23-04-9781845692155.jpg)II. 19.4. Schematic flow diagram of PPCC.
Laboratory investigations were conducted to find a [sorbent](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/sorbent) material for alkali removal at 1400 °C under [PPCC](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/materials-science/coal-combustion) [conditions sufficient](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/sufficient-condition) to fulfil the demands of the gas turbine manufacturers [005Esc]. In laboratory-scale flow channel and [HPMS](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/mass-spectrometry) experiments at 1400 °C, similar to those mentioned above, kaolin- and silica-enriched bauxite have shown the best ability to remove the alkalis sufficiently. The alkalis are bound in a melt–glass phase formed during alkali [sorption](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/sorption). The total NaCl concentration can be reduced to values less than 30 vol. ppb.

Based on the experimental results, the [thermodynamic stability](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/thermodynamic-stability) of the sulphates and other species in the gas turbine was calculated using a three-stage reactor model. The calculations were performed for a typical hard coal (79% C, 5% H, 2% H2O, 7% O2, 1% S, 6% ashes and 0.1% Cl). Figure II.19.5 shows a schematic diagram of the reactor model. [Thermodynamic equilibrium](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/thermodynamic-equilibrium) was calculated for the reactors labelled ‘Combustion chamber’, ‘Hot-gas cleaning’ and ‘Gas turbine’. The boundary conditions for the calculations are given in the scheme. First of all, in the ‘Combustion chamber’ the equilibrium between coal and synthetic air (79 vol.% N2 and 21 vol.% O2) was calculated to obtain an idea of the hot-flue-gas composition leaving the [combustion chamber](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/combustion-chamber). Both the gas phase and the condensed phase from the ‘Combustion chamber’ were taken as input for the following calculations. In ‘Hot-gas cleaning’, thermodynamic equilibrium was calculated using the corresponding boundary conditions. The alkali [partial pressure](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/engineering/partial-pressure) in the resulting gas phase was manually set to 2.4E-8 bar according to the experimentally obtained values. Since all particles and slag droplets should be removed by the liquid-slag separators, only the gas phase was used for the subsequent calculations. Since, in the process, cooling air is added at the entrance of the gas turbine, synthetic air (79 vol.% N2 and 21 vol.% O2) was added to the gas stream entering the ‘Gas turbine’ to reach an overall λ value of 2. Finally, the thermodynamic stability of the sulphates results from equilibrium calculations for the ‘Gas turbine’. The same target calculations were performed as for second-generation [CPFBC](https://www.sciencedirect.com/topics/chemical-engineering/fluidized-bed).
